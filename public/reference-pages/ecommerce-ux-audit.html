You know something is off, but nobody can tell you what

Traffic is fine. The product is fine. The store “looks fine.” And yet buyers stall somewhere between the product page and the order confirmation — and nobody can tell you where. Agencies will sell you a redesign; freelancers will give you opinions. What you actually need is an itemized expert list: exactly what’s wrong, where, why it matters, and what to change first.

That is what a teardown-style audit is for. A diagnosis before fixes — so you spend on the right changes instead of the loudest ones, and so whoever implements them (you, your team, or me) works from evidence instead of guesses.

What does an ecommerce UX audit actually cover?

An ecommerce UX audit covers the full buying journey of your store — homepage, navigation, collections, product pages, cart, checkout, and mobile — reviewed by an expert against 13 areas, with every friction point documented in annotated screenshots and ranked by revenue impact. The deliverable is a written report, not a call and not a slide deck of generic advice.

That is different from an automated audit tool: tools scan code — they don’t experience the store as a buyer does. Different from a redesign quote: the audit works inside your existing theme. And different from an agency retainer: this is a one-time, fixed-price deliverable — one senior operator, no relay.

What it is not: not a speed-test printout, not generic best-practice slides, and not guaranteed lift percentages.

My method: the 13-area teardown

Every audit runs the same discipline: homepage, navigation, collections, product page messaging, product images and video, pricing and offer, reviews and trust, delivery and returns, variants and purchase controls, mobile experience, cart, checkout, and ads and landing-page consistency. Reviewed the way a buyer moves — mobile and desktop — because friction rarely sits where you expect it.

Each finding gets three things: an annotated screenshot so you see exactly what I saw, an explanation of why it costs sales, and the concrete fix. Then everything is ranked by revenue impact — so the report opens with what matters most and you never have to guess where to start. What a finding actually looks like

Every finding in the report follows the same five-field format — Problem, Evidence, Impact, Fix, Priority. The example below is illustrative and anonymized: it shows the format, not a client case. The pattern it describes — delivery information hidden from the buy button — is one that keeps appearing in independent store reviews.

  • — Problem: no delivery or returns information anywhere near the buy button.
  • — Evidence: the product page shows price and add-to-bag; shipping cost and delivery time appear only at checkout. The store’s own policy pages confirm flat-rate shipping and a returns window — buyers just can’t see them where the purchase decision happens.
  • — Impact: buyers decide at the buy button, not at checkout — and hidden shipping cost is one of the most common reasons carts get abandoned. Page-level friction at the purchase decision, touching most buyers.
  • — Fix: one line under the buy button — the store’s real policy, surfaced where it matters (e.g. “Flat $7 shipping · free over $80 · arrives in 3–5 days”), linking to the full policy. Two lines of copy, no redesign.
  • — Priority: P1 — highest expected revenue impact.

Every finding in your report looks like this: the specific page and element, the evidence I saw, why it costs sales, the concrete change — and its rank. Nothing vague, nothing you have to interpret.

Ranked by conversion impact — never by how it looks

The rank answers one question: if you can only fix five things this month, which five move revenue? Each finding is ranked by expected conversion impact — the pages the friction touches, how much buyer traffic reaches them, and how close the friction sits to the purchase. Visual preference plays no part: “this looks dated” is never a finding on its own.

  • — P1 — fix first: friction at the purchase decision itself — the buy-button area, cart, checkout entry — touching most buyers. Hidden shipping costs, missing trust signals where payment happens, broken purchase controls.
  • — P2 — fix next: friction on your highest-traffic pages — homepage, top collections, key product pages — that stalls buyers before they ever reach the decision.
  • — P3 — when there’s time: everything else: low-traffic pages, edge cases, and visual polish. A prettier button never outranks a missing shipping cost.

The report opens with the P1 list — so the first page is where the money is, and nobody has to guess where to start.
